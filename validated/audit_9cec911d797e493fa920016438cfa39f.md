### Title
Sandwich attack on the Uniswap-like AMM AA template due to missing slippage protection combined with attacker-controllable trigger-execution ordering - ([File: test/samples/uniswap_like_market_maker.oscript])

### Summary
The `uniswap_like_market_maker.oscript` autonomous-agent (AA) template shipped with ocore implements a constant-product market maker (`$p = $asset_balance * $bytes_balance`) that lets any unprivileged trigger sender exchange bytes for an asset (and vice versa) at whatever price is implied by the AA's *current* balances, with no minimum-output ("slippage") parameter supplied by the caller. Because AA trigger execution order for units sharing a main-chain index is deterministic but derived from unit `level` (itself chosen by whoever composes the unit via its selected parents), an attacker can order a large front-running trade immediately before, and a back-running reversal immediately after, a victim's exchange trigger — exactly the Uniswap sandwich pattern described in the TrufPartner report, but reachable purely through unit composition rather than mempool manipulation.

### Finding Description
The two "exchange" branches of the AA compute the output amount purely from the pool's own balances at the time the trigger is processed, with no bound supplied by the trigger sender: [1](#0-0) [2](#0-1) 

There is no `trigger.data` field such as `min_amount_out` checked against `$amount` before the payment message is emitted, so the sender has no way to bound how much the pool's `x*y=k` price may have moved between when they decided to trade and when their trigger actually executes.

Execution order for AA triggers is not simply “first submitted, first executed”: triggers are read and executed in an order keyed on `level` (and then unit id/address), both at initial stabilization and at each MCI stabilization pass: [3](#0-2) [4](#0-3) 

`level` is a function of the parents a unit's author chooses when composing it (deeper DAG ancestry ⇒ higher level). An attacker who observes a victim's about-be-broadcast exchange trigger can:
1. Compose and broadcast a large "swap" trigger unit (a big deposit of `base` or of `$asset`) whose parents deliberately exclude the victim's unit, ensuring it gets a lower `level`/is ordered before the victim's trigger once both stabilize.
2. Let the victim's trigger execute against the now-skewed pool ratio, receiving a worse price (materially less output) than expected, since no minimum-output check exists to abort the trade.
3. Compose a reversing trigger unit that explicitly parents the victim's unit (guaranteeing a strictly higher `level`, thus processed after), extracting the value pushed into the pool by the victim, and closing out the temporary position.

This is a direct structural analog of the `TrufPartner.pay` issue: an on-chain automated-market-maker action is executed without any caller-supplied minimum-output/slippage bound, and the surrounding execution-ordering mechanism (here, DAG level/parent selection instead of mempool position) is influenceable by the attacker in a way that lets them guarantee "before" and "after" positions around a specific victim trigger.

### Impact Explanation
Any user of this AMM AA template who calls the "exchange bytes to asset" or "exchange asset to bytes" branch can have their trade sandwiched by an attacker who controls unit composition/parent selection, causing them to receive substantially less than the fair-value output implied by the pool state at the time they decided to trade, while the attacker extracts the difference risk-free (paying only unit/transaction fees). This is a concrete unauthorized value transfer from the victim trader to the attacker via price manipulation of AA-held funds — a fund-loss impact for users of any AA built from this pattern.

### Likelihood Explanation
The template is shipped as an official ocore/oscript-editor example (`test/samples/uniswap_like_market_maker.oscript`, exercised by `test/ojson.test.js`), meaning it is likely to be copied or adapted by third-party AA authors as a starting point for building AMMs on Obyte. The DAG level/parent-selection mechanism used to reorder attacker vs. victim triggers is a normal, permissionless operation available to anyone composing a unit — no special privilege, witness collusion, or network-level attack is required, only the ability to choose parents and timing when broadcasting units, which any wallet already does.

### Recommendation
- Update the AMM template (and document the pattern for AA authors generally) to require an explicit minimum-output parameter in `trigger.data` (e.g., `min_amount`) for both exchange branches, and `bounce()` if the computed `$amount` is below it, mirroring the ratio-check already present in the "invest in MM" branch.
- Document for AA authors that ocore's DAG-based trigger ordering (by `level`, then unit/address) is attacker-influenceable via parent selection and should not be relied upon as a substitute for slippage protection in any AA that performs constant-product or other price-sensitive exchanges.

### Proof of Concept
Conceptual reproduction using the template:
1. Attacker observes a soon-to-be-broadcast victim unit that will trigger the "exchange bytes to asset" branch of the AMM AA with a large `base` amount.
2. Attacker composes and broadcasts unit `U_front` sending a large `base` payment to the AA via the same branch, using parents that do not descend from the victim's unit, so `U_front` gets a lower `level`.
3. Once both `U_front` and the victim's unit `U_victim` are included and reach the same/adjacent stabilized MCI, `aa_composer.js`'s `handleAATriggers`/`main_chain.js` ordering (`ORDER BY ... level, unit, address`) processes `U_front` before `U_victim`, skewing `$asset_balance`/`$bytes_balance` so `U_victim`'s `$amount` (asset out) is computed at a worse rate — with no minimum-output check to reject this (lines 102-123 of the oscript).
4. Attacker composes unit `U_back` sending the acquired `$asset` back through the "exchange asset to bytes" branch, explicitly using `U_victim` (or a descendant) as a parent so `U_back` gets a higher `level` and executes after, recovering `base` at the now-favorable ratio.
5. Net effect: attacker profits in `base`/`$asset` at the expense of the victim's trade, with no interaction restricted by any privileged role.
