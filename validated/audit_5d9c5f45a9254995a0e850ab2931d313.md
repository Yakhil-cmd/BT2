### Title
Adversarial attestor can permanently block delivery of `spender_attested` assets to a legitimate counterparty, freezing AA/user payment obligations - (File: validation.js)

### Summary
The Cooler bug lets a lender revert an ERC-20 `transferFrom` (via a blocklist) so that a borrower's repayment can never be delivered, forcing an unwanted default. In `ocore`, the analogous mechanism is the `spender_attested` asset feature: every output address of a payment in such an asset must currently be on the attestor's published attestation list, or the *entire* payment message is rejected as invalid. Since the attestor is an independent, self-interested party who is not required to attest any particular address, an attestor can simply never attest (or can revoke attestation of) a legitimate counterparty's address, permanently blocking any transfer to it — including payouts owed by an AA or another user — with no way for the payer to force completion.

### Finding Description
When an asset is defined with `spender_attested: true`, `validatePayment` requires that at least one author of the transaction is currently attested, and `validatePaymentInputsAndOutputs` additionally requires that **every output address** be attested at the moment of validation, otherwise the whole payment fails: [1](#0-0)

The attestation state is entirely controlled by the attestor(s) named in the asset definition and can be updated at any time via an `asset_attestors` message: [2](#0-1) 

Because attestation is checked against the *current* stable state (`last_ball_mci`) rather than fixed at the time the payer composed/broadcast the transaction, an attestor who is a counterparty to a deal (analogous to the "lender" in the Cooler report) can simply refuse to attest, or de-attest, the recipient address that is supposed to receive an owed payment. Any unit paying that asset to that address will be rejected by every validating node — this is a deterministic, consensus-consistent rejection, so the payer's only escape route is to never complete the obligation to that recipient. This is functionally identical to the Cooler scenario: the receiving party (a "lender"-like role) makes itself unable to receive the expected fund transfer, and the paying party has no way around it, resulting in the obligation being defaulted rather than fulfilled. This same choke point applies to AA-controlled funds: an AA holding a `spender_attested` asset that is supposed to release funds to a user upon meeting on-chain conditions (e.g., completing a swap, redeeming collateral, winning an auction) cannot force delivery if the attestor withholds attestation of the recipient, and the AA’s attempt to pay out will simply bounce or fail (`aa_composer.js` `bounce()`), leaving the AA’s funds effectively stuck/frozen relative to that recipient.

### Impact Explanation
- A counterparty acting as (or colluding with) the asset's attestor can unilaterally and permanently prevent delivery of funds/asset units owed to a specific address, defeating any escrow/vesting/repayment-style logic built on top of a `spender_attested` asset.
- For AA-based flows, this can cause AA fund freezing: the AA cannot complete its payout obligation to the blocked address, and depending on AA logic, the AA may bounce repeatedly (burning bounce fees) or become stuck holding assets that can never legitimately leave for that recipient.
- This is not a hypothetical "if a user does something wrong" case — the attestor's discretion over attestation is a first-class, permissionless(after being named)-controlled lever that can be exercised against any specific address at will, at zero cost, at any time, including after a deal is set up in good faith.

### Likelihood Explanation
Any asset with `spender_attested: true` is subject to this control (this is normal, permitted asset configuration, not requiring any special privilege beyond being the attestor named at asset-definition time). Any AA or user relying on such an asset for conditional, escrow-like, or repayment-like flows to a specific counterparty is exposed. The check occurs on every payment message using the asset, so the attack surface (an unattested/de-attested output address) is trivially reachable by an ordinary payment or AA payout attempting to pay that asset to the targeted recipient.

### Recommendation
- For flows where a specific payout must be guaranteed to a counterparty (e.g., AA-driven repayments/escrows), avoid using `spender_attested` assets as the settlement asset, or design the AA/contract logic so that failure to deliver (a bounce) does not itself trigger an adverse state transition (e.g., "default") against the payer.
- Consider caching/pinning the attestation check against the state at which the obligation was created (e.g., snapshot attestation at contract creation time) rather than re-checking current attestation at the moment of every settlement payment, so a counterparty cannot retroactively deny an already-agreed transfer.
- Document explicitly for AA/dApp developers building payment/escrow AAs that `spender_attested` assets carry this griefing risk and should not be used for time-critical or obligatory payouts to a single named recipient without a fallback/dispute mechanism.

### Proof of Concept
1. Party A creates an asset `X` with `spender_attested: true` and attestor `T` (could be A themselves, or a party colluding with a counterparty who will later act adversarially).
2. Party A and Party B agree that on fulfilment of a condition, Party A (or an AA) will send `X` to Party B's address `addrB`. `addrB` is attested by `T` at the time of the agreement.
3. Before Party A/AA sends the settlement payment, `T` posts an updated `asset_attestors` list that no longer includes `addrB` (`validation.js` `validateAttestorListUpdate` path, stored via `writer.js` `asset_attestors` case).
4. Party A/AA attempts the payment message with output to `addrB`; `validatePaymentInputsAndOutputs` calls `storage.filterAttestedAddresses` and finds `addrB` not attested, returning `"some output addresses are not attested"` (`validation.js:2637-2638`), causing the unit to be rejected everywhere.
5. Party B never receives the owed asset; if this was implemented as an AA-based conditional payout, the AA either fails to pay (bounces) or is stuck with `X` allocated to `addrB` that it can never legitimately deliver, mirroring the Cooler `repay()` default scenario where the payer cannot fulfil the obligation due to the counterparty-controlled block.
