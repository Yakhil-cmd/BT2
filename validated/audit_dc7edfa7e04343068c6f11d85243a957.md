### Title
Hash-lock address authentifier (`case 'hash'`) enforces no minimum entropy or key-stretching on the preimage - ([File: definition.js])

### Summary
Obyte address/asset definitions support a `hash` authentifier type that unlocks funds by revealing a preimage whose single-round SHA-256 digest matches a value baked into the definition. Because the check uses a single, unsalted, non-stretched SHA-256 hash (exactly the "insufficient entropy" pattern described in the parsel advisory, CWE-331), any secret chosen with low entropy (e.g. a short numeric code, guessable phrase, or a value generated by another weak process) can be brute-forced offline at native SHA-256 speed, letting an unrelated party satisfy the authentifier and spend the funds.

The validity of a spend authorized this way depends entirely on the secrecy/entropy of the preimage supplied in `assocAuthentifiers[path]`. Unlike password-hashing schemes, `crypto.createHash("sha256")` performs exactly one hashing round with no salt and no configurable work factor (no PBKDF2/bcrypt/scrypt/Argon2). This is precisely the weakness flagged in the parsel advisory: hashing a possibly low-entropy secret through a single SHA-256 round provides no protection against brute-force guessing of that secret, since SHA-256 is intentionally fast and offers no key-stretching.

Any unprivileged party who knows or observes an address/asset definition using this authentifier type (definitions are public once used on the DAG, and are also disclosable in advance for smart-contract/HTLC-style flows) can attempt to brute force the preimage. Because there's no entropy/length floor enforced by the validator (`assocAuthentifiers[path]` only needs to be a non-empty string), a definition author (or a poorly-implemented wallet/AA that generates the secret with low entropy) can inadvertently create an address that's crackable by anyone, not just the intended cosigner.

### Impact Explanation
If a `hash` authentifier is used to lock a hash-lock address/asset condition (e.g., swap/HTLC-style condition) and the underlying secret was chosen with insufficient entropy, any attacker capable of enumerating candidate secrets and computing `SHA256(candidate)` can produce a valid authentifier and satisfy that clause of the definition, resulting in unauthorized spending of the locked funds. This is a concrete "unauthorized spending" impact reachable by an unprivileged unit poster who simply guesses the preimage and posts a spending unit with the matching authentifier.

### Likelihood Explanation
Exploitability is entirely conditioned on the entropy of the externally-chosen secret, which ocore does not control or validate (no minimum length/entropy check on `assocAuthentifiers[path]` or on `args.hash`). If any wallet, AA-driven flow, or dApp built on top of ocore generates or accepts low-entropy secrets for `hash` conditions (e.g., short PINs, dictionary words), the lack of stretching makes brute force trivial and cheap. Likelihood is moderate to high in practice wherever this rarely-used authentifier type is combined with weak secret generation, but it requires a specific usage pattern (hash-lock definitions) rather than being universally exploitable across all ocore addresses.

### Recommendation
- Where the `hash` authentifier is used for security-sensitive locking (e.g., atomic-swap/HTLC use cases), require the preimage to have a documented minimum entropy (e.g., ≥128 bits of randomness) and reject use of the primitive for low-entropy human-memorable secrets.
- Consider adding an explicit minimum length/entropy check on `assocAuthentifiers[path]` in `definition.js`, and clearly document that `hash` is not a password-hashing/KDF primitive — it must only be fed high-entropy, cryptographically random preimages.
- Do not add key-stretching to `hash` itself (that would break existing definitions), but ensure wallets/dApps generating such secrets use CSPRNG-derived high-entropy values, and add documentation/lint warnings for definitions employing `hash` with likely-weak preimages.

### Proof of Concept
1. Alice creates an address definition `['hash', {algo: 'sha256', hash: BASE64(SHA256("1234"))}]` (or any low-entropy secret), intending only a trusted counterparty to know the code.
2. The definition (and thus the hash) becomes visible once referenced/used in a unit on the public DAG, or is disclosed in advance as part of a swap protocol.
3. Mallory, an unprivileged network participant, iterates over candidate 4-digit codes, computing `crypto.createHash("sha256").update(candidate,"utf8").digest("base64")` (as done in `definition.js:763`) until it matches `args.hash`.
4. Mallory submits a spending unit supplying the discovered preimage as `assocAuthentifiers[path]`, satisfying the authentifier check at `definition.js:763` and spending the funds without Alice's/the intended counterparty's consent.
