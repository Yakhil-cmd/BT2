## Title
First-depositor price manipulation + rounding drain in the `uniswap_like_market_maker.oscript` AMM pattern - (`test/samples/uniswap_like_market_maker.oscript`)

### Summary
The bundled Uniswap-style AA template lets the very first liquidity provider set an arbitrary, unchecked exchange ratio between `base` (bytes) and a custom asset, and then uses integer-rounded constant-product math (`round($p / balance[...])`) to compute swap outputs. Because ocore assets carry no notion of "decimals" — their effective granularity is whatever cap/typical-trade-size the issuer chooses — a pool seeded with a tiny amount of a low-supply asset against a much larger byte reserve reproduces the same class of rounding breakdown as the DODO GSP report: an attacker can trade a minimal amount of the scarce asset and extract a large share of the byte reserve, draining the pool and making all previously issued MM shares worthless.

### Finding Description
The AA template composes a constant-product pool between `base` and `$asset`:

- Initial deposit sets `$issue_amount = balance[base]` and skips the ratio check entirely: [1](#0-0) 
- Later deposits are checked against the current ratio, and swaps use a naive `x*y=k` formula with `round()`: [2](#0-1)  and [3](#0-2) 
- Divestment pays out a proportional share of both reserves: [4](#0-3) 

Two facts in the core validation/formula layer make this exploitable without any special privilege:

1. Assets have no enforced minimum cap or "precision" relationship to `base`. `validateAssetDefinition` only checks `isPositiveInteger(payload.cap) && payload.cap <= constants.MAX_CAP`; there is no lower bound and no relationship enforced between an asset's cap/granularity and byte amounts: [5](#0-4) 
2. All AA arithmetic (`/`, `*`, `round`) is performed with `Decimal` and then rounded to an integer amount before being placed into a payment output, exactly the kind of division/rounding step that produced the GSP bug: [6](#0-5) 

Because the first depositor's contribution defines the pool's entire price curve with **no minimum-liquidity check and no sanity check on the initial ratio**, any AA trigger sender (including the attacker) can become the first depositor and seed the pool with an extremely lopsided ratio (e.g., 1 unit of `$asset` against a large `base` reserve). From that point, the constant-product formula `$p = $asset_balance * $bytes_balance` combined with `round($p / balance[...])` means that adding even one more unit of the scarce asset causes a disproportionately large swing in the computed `$new_bytes_balance`, letting the attacker extract a large fraction of the byte reserve for a negligible asset cost — mirroring the DODO GSP report where a lower-precision base asset caused `_QUOTE_TARGET_` to round to 0 and let an attacker drain the pool for a single unit of the quote asset.

### Impact Explanation
An attacker who is the first (or an early) depositor into such a pool can permanently skew the price curve, then drain the byte reserve (or the paired asset reserve) via one or two minimal-cost swap triggers. All bytes and/or assets held by the AA — belonging to any other users who deposited or will deposit — are stolen, and outstanding `mm_asset` shares become worthless, since `divest` pays out `round($investor_share * balance[...])` against an already-drained reserve. This is a direct, unauthorized transfer of AA-held funds triggerable by a single posted unit, matching the "AA fund loss" impact category.

### Likelihood Explanation
This requires no special privilege: any address can send the initializing/investing trigger unit to the AA (the "invest in MM" branch has no gate other than sending `>1e5` bytes and some amount of `$asset`), and any address can subsequently send an "exchange" trigger. Any AA author copying/adapting this widely-referenced sample pattern (or MM/DEX AAs built on the same idea) without adding minimum-liquidity checks and ratio sanity bounds is exposed. The lack of any protocol-level enforcement of asset "precision"/minimum cap (see `validateAssetDefinition`) means the AA author cannot rely on the platform to prevent this class of asymmetric-reserve rounding bug.

### Recommendation
- Require a minimum initial liquidity deposit (in both `base` and `$asset` terms) before allowing the "initial deposit" branch to set the price, and/or lock the very first LP's shares for a minimum period, similar to standard Uniswap V2 mitigations.
- Reject deposits/trades if either reserve side, after the trade, would fall below a sane minimum floor relative to the other side, preventing a near-zero-reserve side from being manipulated by minimal amounts.
- When computing `round($p / balance[...])`, additionally validate that the resulting swap amount does not exceed a fraction of the existing reserve (e.g., slippage/impact cap) so a single trade cannot capture a disproportionate share of the pool.
- Document, for AA authors modeling AMMs on ocore, that asset "precision" is not implicit and must be reasoned about explicitly (no equivalent of ERC-20 `decimals()` exists), since mismatched typical unit sizes between `base` and a custom asset reproduce the exact rounding class described in the referenced report.

### Proof of Concept
1. Deploy the AA from `test/samples/uniswap_like_market_maker.oscript`, with `$asset` being an existing divisible asset with a small effective supply/typical trade unit (e.g., cap = 1000).
2. Attacker sends the first "invest in MM" trigger with `trigger.output[[asset=base]] = 1,000,000` bytes and `trigger.output[[asset=$asset]] = 1` unit of `$asset`. Because `$asset_balance == 0` before this trigger, the check at line 44 (`if ($expected_asset_amount != trigger.output[[asset=$asset]]) bounce(...)`) is skipped entirely (branch at lines 38-41), and `$issue_amount = balance[base] = 1,000,000` MM shares are minted to the attacker for 1 unit of `$asset` and 1,000,000 bytes — the attacker fully controls the price curve.
3. Attacker (or a colluding second address) then sends an "exchange asset to bytes" trigger with `trigger.output[[asset=$asset]] = 1` additional unit. `$asset_balance` doubles from 1 to 2, so `$p / balance[$asset]` computed via `round($p/balance[$asset])` roughly halves `$new_bytes_balance`, and `$amount = $bytes_balance - $new_bytes_balance` pays out close to half of the entire byte reserve (≈500,000 bytes) for the cost of a single unit of `$asset`.
4. Repeating similar minimal-cost trades continues to drain the remaining byte reserve, after which the original/legitimate LP's `mm_asset` shares (from the `divest` branch, lines 67-100) redeem to near-zero `base`, replicating the "pool drained, shares worthless" outcome of the referenced report.
