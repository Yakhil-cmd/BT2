The core defect in the Admidio report is a **CSRF** bug: a state-changing HTTP endpoint relies on ambient browser authority (session cookies with `SameSite=Lax`) instead of an explicit, unforgeable per-action credential, letting a third-party page silently trigger a privileged password reset while riding an administrator's authenticated session.

This bug class does not have a structural analog in the Polkadot SDK's extrinsic-dispatch model. Every state-changing FRAME call is authorized either by `ensure_signed`/`ensure_root`/custom `EnsureOrigin` checks against a cryptographically signed extrinsic, or by an explicit proof (e.g. `pallet_session::set_keys` requiring an `ownership_proof_is_valid` check as seen at [1](#0-0)  ). There is no browser-cookie-like ambient credential that a third party can coerce a victim into unknowingly attaching to a forged request — every transaction must be explicitly signed with the caller's private key before it is accepted by validation, and the `people` pallet's transaction extension explicitly binds the origin to alias/identity transformations with proof-of-key material rather than session state, as seen at [2](#0-1) . The closest conceptually-adjacent permissionless flow, `pallet_recovery::initiate_attempt`, is intentionally open to any signed caller but is gated by an economic deposit and multi-friend threshold approval rather than any implicit/ambient credential, as documented at [3](#0-2)  and implemented at [4](#0-3) , which is a fundamentally different (and already-analyzed/documented) trust model, not a missing-CSRF-check analog.

Since Polkadot SDK's dispatch pipeline has no session/cookie-based implicit authentication surface for a CSRF-style forged-request attack to exploit, forcing this bug class onto FRAME/XCM would be an unsupported analogy rather than a genuine, demonstrable vulnerability.

### Malicious Recovery Attempts

Initializing the recovery process for a recoverable account is open and
permissionless. However, the recovery deposit is an economic deterrent that
should disincentivize would-be attackers from trying to maliciously recover
accounts.

The recovery deposit can always be claimed by the account which is trying
to be recovered. In the case of a malicious recovery attempt, the account
owner who still has access to their account can claim the deposit and
essentially punish the malicious user.

Furthermore, the malicious recovery attempt can only be successful if the
attacker is also able to get enough friends to vouch for the recovery attempt.
In the case where the account owner prevents a malicious recovery process,
this pallet makes it near-zero cost to re-configure the recovery settings and
remove/replace friends who are acting inappropriately.
