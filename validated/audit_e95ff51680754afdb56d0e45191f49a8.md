### Title
Arbiter contract acceptance signature is bound only to `title`, not to the full contract hash, enabling signature replay across different contract terms - (File: `wallet.js`)

### Summary
`ocore`'s arbiter-contract flow lets a peer cryptographically "accept" a contract offer by signing a message and sending it back to the offeror's device. The offeror's handler for `arbiter_contract_response` verifies only that the signed string equals the contract's free-text `title` field, not the full contract hash (which is what actually commits to all economic terms: amount, asset, arbiter, text, addresses). Because `title` is set by the contract creator and is not guaranteed to be unique or bound to a specific contract instance, a previously captured, validly-signed "accept" message can be replayed against a different contract record (with a different `hash`) that happens to share the same `title`, silently promoting that unrelated/different contract to `accepted` status without the peer ever authenticating its actual terms. This mirrors the reported bug class: an authenticated request (a signature) that is not bound to enough context/state (here: the contract hash/version) so it can be replayed to force an unintended state transition.

### Finding Description
The acceptance/decline flow works as follows:

- The peer signs `objContract.title` as the "signed message" when responding to an offer [1](#0-0) .
- The offeror's device receives the response over `arbiter_contract_response` and validates it:
```
signed_message.validateSignedMessage(db, objSignedMessage, objContract.peer_address, function(err) {
    if (err || objSignedMessage.authors[0].address !== objContract.peer_address || objSignedMessage.signed_message != objContract.title)
        return callbacks.ifError("wrong contract signature");
    processResponse(objSignedMessage);
});
``` [2](#0-1) 

The only content-binding check performed on the signed payload is equality with `objContract.title` — a short, attacker-controllable, free-text field of the *specific* local contract record looked up by `body.hash` [3](#0-2) . Nothing ties the signature to the contract's `hash` (which is derived from all the economically meaningful fields such as `amount`, `asset`, `arbiter_address`, `text`, `my_address`, `peer_address`, per `createAndSend`'s use of `getHash(objContract)` [4](#0-3) ), nor to any nonce or contract-specific identifier, nor to freshness (no last-ball/mci binding is enforced for this particular signed string as it is not run through the network-aware `last_ball_unit` path used elsewhere for `is_valid_signed_package`).

The status state machine that gates acceptance is also permissive on replays for pending contracts:
```
var isAllowed = objContract.status === "pending" || (objContract.status === 'accepted' && body.status === 'accepted');
``` [5](#0-4) 
This only checks the *target* contract's own status, not whether the presented signature was ever produced for *that* contract.

Because a device can receive/create multiple different `wallet_arbiter_contracts` rows (different `hash`, different terms) that share an identical `title` string (the offeror fully controls `title`, `text`, `amount`, `asset` when creating an offer via `createAndSend`), a signature the peer produced once for one contract's `title` can be replayed to flip a second, different contract (different hash/terms) from `pending` to `accepted`, since the verification code never re-derives or compares `objContract.hash`.

### Impact Explanation
An attacker who controls the offeror's side of a device pairing/contract relationship (a normal, unprivileged wallet-to-wallet flow reachable by any "contract" counterparty) can:
1. Send the victim two arbiter-contract offers with the same `title` but different terms (e.g., different `amount`, `asset`, or `arbiter_address` chosen to favor the attacker or a colluding arbiter).
2. Wait for (or induce) the victim to accept the benign-looking offer, capturing the resulting signed acceptance message.
3. Replay that signature against the second, malicious offer's `hash`.
4. The offeror's local state now shows the malicious contract as `accepted` by the peer, which drives subsequent flows: `shareContractToCosigners`, shared multisig address creation, and eventual fund commitment/payment logic (`complete`) — all premised on the peer having consented to *those specific* terms.

This can result in unauthorized/forced acceptance of contract terms the peer never actually agreed to, and downstream loss of committed funds when the multisig shared address is later funded and disputes/arbitration play out under the attacker-chosen terms. This satisfies the "concrete unauthorized spending / fund loss" bar for Medium/High severity.

### Likelihood Explanation
Requires the attacker to be a contract counterparty (unprivileged, no special node/hub access) who controls the offer content (`title`, `text`, `amount`, `asset`) and who can get the victim to sign an acceptance once (a normal user action in this feature). No mempool sniffing, hub compromise, or private-key theft is needed — the signed message is delivered directly to the offeror's device as part of normal protocol flow, and the offeror is the party performing the (insufficient) verification. Because contract acceptance is a routine, user-facing action, and because nothing in the code enforces `title` uniqueness or binds the signature to `hash`, exploitation is realistic whenever a user interacts with more than one contract from the same or colluding counterparties, or where the counterparty proposes a `title` matching a prior contract by construction.

### Recommendation
Bind the peer's acceptance/decline signature to the full contract `hash` (or another value that already commits to all contract terms) instead of the free-text `title`:
- Change `respond()` to sign `objContract.hash` (or a canonical hash of all economic fields) rather than `objContract.title`.
- Change the verification in `wallet.js`'s `arbiter_contract_response` handler to check `objSignedMessage.signed_message === objContract.hash` (and `body.hash === objContract.hash`) instead of comparing to `title`.
- Additionally consider adding freshness (e.g., embedding `objContract.hash` plus a client-generated nonce, or requiring `last_ball_unit`) so that even hash-bound signatures cannot be replayed if the contract is later mutated/represented under a new record.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker (offeror) creates Contract A: `{title: "Freelance job", text: "Pay 1 GBYTE for design work", amount: 1e9, asset: "base", arbiter_address: X}` and sends it to Victim via `arbiter_contract_offer` [6](#0-5) .
2. Victim reviews Contract A, agrees, and calls `respond(hash_A, "accepted", ...)`, which signs `objContract.title = "Freelance job"` and sends it back [7](#0-6) .
3. Attacker independently creates Contract B and stores it locally on the victim's device by sending a second offer with the **same title** `"Freelance job"` but `amount: 100e9`, a different `arbiter_address` controlled by a colluding arbiter, and different `text`. This gets a different `hash_B` [8](#0-7) .
4. Attacker relays the previously captured signed message (from step 2) back to the victim's device as an `arbiter_contract_response` for `hash: hash_B`, `status: "accepted"`.
5. `wallet.js`'s handler validates the signature against `objContract.peer_address` and checks only `objSignedMessage.signed_message != objContract.title` — since both contracts share `title = "Freelance job"`, this check passes even though the victim never signed anything referencing Contract B's actual (much larger) `amount` or its `arbiter_address` [2](#0-1) .
6. Contract B is marked `accepted` on the victim's device without genuine consent, and the flow proceeds to `shareContractToCosigners`/shared-address creation, exposing the victim to loss of funds under terms they never actually agreed to.

Note: I was unable to locate the exact call site where `signMessage()` produces the `signedMessageBase64` passed into `respond()` (likely in a UI-layer file outside indexed scope), and could not fully confirm every field included in `getHash()`; these are noted as unverified details, but the core issue — verification comparing only against `title` rather than the full contract hash — is directly confirmed in `wallet.js` and `arbiter_contract.js`.
