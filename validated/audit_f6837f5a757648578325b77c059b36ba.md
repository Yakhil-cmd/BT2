### Title
Hash-preimage address-unlock (`['hash', …]`) is not bound to a specific redeemer, enabling front‑running of secret-based conditions - (File: `definition.js`)

### Summary
The reported `NativeTokenPaymentEnforcer` bug is a class of vulnerability where a shared, reusable authorization artifact (a payment delegation blob) is not cryptographically bound to the specific party that is supposed to redeem it, so any third party that observes it in flight can race the intended redeemer and consume it first. `ocore`'s address-definition language contains an analogous primitive: the `['hash', {algo, hash}]` op, which unlocks spending simply by revealing a preimage string as an authentifier, with no requirement that the unlocking author also prove control of any address/pubkey [1](#0-0) . When this op is used as (or as part of) an address's spending condition without being combined with a `['sig', …]`/`['address', …]` check on the same branch, whoever first observes the revealed secret (e.g., in an atomic-swap counter-party's broadcasted claim unit, or in any other unit that discloses the preimage) can immediately compose and broadcast a competing unit from the same address using the same secret, racing the legitimate owner to spend the funds.

### Finding Description
`validateAuthentifiers` in `definition.js` implements the `hash` op verification: it accepts any string supplied at the corresponding authentifier path and checks only that `sha256(secret) === expected_hash` [2](#0-1) . Nothing in this check (or in the earlier structural validation of the `hash` op in `validateDefinition`, lines 252-267 [1](#0-0) ) ties the successful unlock to the identity of the unit's author — the same secret authorizes spending regardless of who reveals it in a valid unit.

This mirrors the `NativeTokenPaymentEnforcer` flaw: there, the payment delegation (`_args`) is a self-contained artifact that authorizes payment to any redeemer who presents it, and the fix required *mandatorily* binding the delegation to the specific redeemer via `ArgsEqualityCheckEnforcer` with the delegation hash. In `ocore`, the `hash` op is the corresponding "self-contained secret" that authorizes spending for *whoever* presents it, and the protocol does not mandate — it only optionally allows via `and`/`or` composition — that it be combined with a signature/address check that binds the unlock to a specific intended party.

In practice this is exploited in HTLC-style atomic swaps: Party A creates an address unlockable by revealing preimage `S` whose hash `H` was pre-shared, intending only Party B (who already knows `S` from the off-chain protocol) to claim it. The moment B broadcasts a unit revealing `S` (e.g., to claim funds on the other chain, or in the DAG itself before stabilization), any observer who is capable of racing units can construct a *different* unit from the same address, plug in the leaked `S`, and try to get their own conflicting unit stabilized instead of B's, since the DAG allows multiple conflicting units from an address and only one becomes `sequence='good'` once the fork resolves. `ocore` never requires that the `hash` unlocking branch also be gated by a signature of the intended party, so the protocol/design pattern is inherently races-prone for anyone composing address definitions that rely solely on `hash` without pairing it with `sig`/`address`.

### Impact Explanation
If an attacker races and wins the fork resolution (e.g., by getting their competing unit onto the best/main chain first via better connectivity or witness placement), they can steal the entire balance guarded by the hash-lock, resulting in concrete unauthorized spending/fund loss for the legitimate claimant — directly analogous to the impact category "concrete unauthorized spending" called out in the validation rules.

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood depends entirely on how users/wallets choose to compose address definitions: `ocore` does not forbid a `hash`-only (or `hash`-OR'd) spending branch that lacks a companion signature check, and revealing the secret is an unavoidable step of any hash-lock based swap protocol built on top of `ocore`. Any unprivileged unit poster who is watching the network for units revealing a matching preimage can attempt to front-run, making this reachable without any privileged position, purely from observing broadcast (unstable) units — consistent with the "unprivileged unit poster" reachable path required by the validation rules.

### Recommendation
- At the protocol/documentation level, `ocore` should discourage (or the `validateDefinition` structural check should reject) definitions in which a `hash` op is not combined (via `and`) with an `address`/`sig` check that binds the spend to the intended redeemer's key, similar to how the delegation-framework fix made `ArgsEqualityCheckEnforcer` mandatory rather than optional.
- Where full mitigation in the core validation logic is not feasible (since `hash`-only branches may be intentionally desired for specific legitimate use-cases, e.g. lottery/reveal schemes), the wallet/UX layers that generate atomic-swap and hash-lock addresses should be updated to always AND the `hash` condition with an `address`/`sig` check for the intended claimant, closing the race window described above.

### Proof of Concept
1. Party A defines an address with `['hash', {algo: 'sha256', hash: H}]` as the sole spending condition (no accompanying `address`/`sig` branch), and funds it, intending only Party B — who alone knows preimage `S` — to claim it.
2. Party B broadcasts unit `U1` from that address with an authentifier equal to `S` at the corresponding path, satisfying `validateAuthentifiers`'s `hash` case [2](#0-1) .
3. Before `U1` stabilizes, an attacker who has observed `S` (from `U1` itself while it is still unstable, or from a related chain in an atomic swap) constructs a conflicting unit `U2` from the same address, using the same authentifier `S`, redirecting outputs to their own address.
4. Because `ocore` allows multiple non-serial units from the same address until one becomes stable, and the `hash` check has no dependency on the author's identity, `U2` is equally valid per `validateAuthentifiers`; whichever of `U1`/`U2` ends up serial/stable determines who actually receives the funds, allowing the attacker to race and potentially win control of the funds intended for Party B.
